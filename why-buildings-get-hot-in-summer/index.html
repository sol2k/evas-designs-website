Why buildings get hot in summer — and how smart design cools them.

A building that bakes all May and June is not bad luck — it is physics. Roofs soak up sun, walls store heat, glass lets it pour in and dark colours hold it. The good news: every one of these has a design answer. This guide explains why buildings overheat in Indian summers and four ideas we use to cool them down, before the AC bill arrives.

01 — The quick answer Why buildings overheat

Buildings get hot because they absorb more sun than they can release.

Concrete, brick and glass all behave badly under an Indian summer sun — each in its own way. Here is where the heat actually comes from.

Heat source How it heats your building First design fix
The roof A bare flat roof takes direct sun all day, heats up like a tawa and radiates that heat into the rooms below for hours after sunset. Cool roof coating, shading, insulation or a green roof layer.
West & south walls Afternoon sun strikes walls at a low angle and soaks them for hours, turning them into slow radiators through the evening. External shading, greenery and light finishes on hot faces.
Glass areas Plain glass lets sunlight pour straight in and then traps the heat inside — the greenhouse effect, working against you all afternoon. Smaller west openings, double glazing and external shading.
Dark colours Dark roofs and facades absorb most of the solar energy that lands on them instead of reflecting it away. Light colours and heat-reflective coatings.
Trapped hot air Homes without cross-ventilation hold the day's heat inside long after the outside air has cooled. Planned openings and ventilation paths.

The short version

Heat enters through the roof, the west walls and the glass — and gets trapped by dark surfaces and still air.

The expensive mistake

Treating heat as an AC problem only. Oversized air conditioning fights symptoms; design removes the cause.

The Evas approach

We design heat out of the building first — orientation, shading, materials, glazing — then size cooling for what remains.

02 — Idea one Vertical gardens

Let plants take the heat instead of your walls.

A vertical garden is not just decoration — it is a living sunshade pinned to the hottest face of your building.

How it cools

Plants work against heat in two ways at once. Foliage shades the wall, so direct sun never reaches the plaster or concrete. Then, as leaves release moisture, the air layer next to the building cools down — a natural effect called evapotranspiration. A green wall on a west or south face keeps the structure measurably cooler, which means less heat conducted indoors every single afternoon.

  • Best on west and south walls, balconies and boundary walls
  • Shades the surface and cools the air next to it, together
  • Improves air quality and softens the facade visually
  • Needs correct waterproofing, structure and drip irrigation

Where we use it

For independent floors and villas in Gurgaon, we often combine a green boundary wall with planter-based screens on hot balconies — the building gets shade without losing light. For builder floors, even a single planted trellis outside a west-facing bedroom window changes how that room feels at 5 pm in June.

  • Green boundary walls that cool the whole plot edge
  • Balcony trellises and planter screens for hot windows
  • Facade-mounted systems for larger walls and villas
  • Plant palettes chosen for NCR heat and water realities

Design note: a vertical garden done badly — wrong plants, no irrigation, moisture against the wall — fails within a season. Our building architect in Gurgaon team plans greenery as part of the facade system, not as an afterthought.

03 — Idea two Solar parking sheds

Shade your cars and power your home with the same roof.

Most parking sheds are dead weight — a tin sheet that blocks sun and nothing else. A solar canopy does both jobs at once.

Two problems, one structure

A car parked under the summer sun becomes an oven, and a plain shed only solves half of that problem. Replace the sheet with solar panels and the same canopy keeps cars cool while generating electricity or charging batteries for the house. The parking bay stops being wasted space and starts paying rent.

  • Cars stay dramatically cooler than in open sun
  • Panels generate power or charge home batteries
  • Works for villas, builder floors, offices and farmhouses
  • A working asset, not just an expense

What needs designing

The canopy has to be engineered, not improvised: tilt angle for generation, height for car clearance, structure for wind loads, gutters for monsoon rain and clean cable routing into the home's system. Done well, it reads as part of the architecture — a sharp, floating plane rather than a bolt-on.

  • Tilt and orientation planned for maximum generation
  • Structure designed for wind, rain and load
  • Integrated wiring, inverter and battery placement
  • Detailed to look like architecture, not an add-on

Design note: the same logic scales up — solar canopies over terraces give top floors shade and power together. Ask us during a site visit what your roof or parking bay could realistically generate.

04 — Idea three Light colours & cool roofs

The cheapest cooling upgrade is a bucket of the right paint.

Colour is not just aesthetics — it is a thermal decision. Light surfaces reflect the sun; dark ones drink it.

Why light colours win

Dark surfaces absorb most of the solar energy that lands on them; light and reflective surfaces bounce much of it back. On a roof, that difference is enormous — a white or cool-coated surface can run dramatically cooler than bare concrete under the same sun, and every degree the surface stays cooler is a degree less conducted into the rooms below.

  • White or light roof finishes reflect solar heat
  • Heat-reflective cool roof coatings go further
  • Top-floor rooms feel the difference first
  • Low cost compared to almost any other fix

Beyond the roof

The same principle runs through the whole exterior: lighter wall paints, paler cladding and reflective finishes on sun-facing elevations all reduce absorbed heat. It pairs naturally with orientation planning from our east-facing vs west-facing buildings guide — the hottest faces deserve the lightest finishes.

  • Lightest finishes reserved for west and south faces
  • Reflective exterior paints and claddings
  • Works on new builds and existing buildings alike
  • Combine with shading for the full effect

Design note: material choice matters as much as colour — walling, insulation and surface finish all change how heat moves. Our building materials comparison for Indian homes breaks those choices down in detail.

05 — Idea four Rethinking glass

In Gurgaon's summer, plain glass is a heater you paid extra for.

Floor-to-ceiling glass looks premium in brochures. Under an NCR afternoon sun, it behaves like a greenhouse panel bolted to your living room.

The greenhouse problem

Plain single glass lets sunlight pass straight through and then traps the resulting heat indoors. In Gurgaon and Delhi NCR summers — especially on west and south faces — large untreated glass areas can push room temperatures up fast and keep air conditioning running at full tilt. Less glass in the wrong place is often the smartest decision a facade can make.

  • Sunlight enters freely, heat gets trapped inside
  • West and south glass suffers most in NCR summers
  • Big glass loads the AC and fades interiors
  • Right-sizing openings is the first fix

Glass, but smarter

Where a view or daylight genuinely calls for glazing, we make it earn its place: double-glazed or low-e units, external shading such as chajjas, fins and screens, and opening sizes matched to orientation. The result keeps the light and the view without importing the heat — the difference between a glass box and a designed facade.

  • Double glazing and low-e glass where glazing is needed
  • External shading beats internal blinds every time
  • Window sizes tuned to each orientation
  • Cross-ventilation planned alongside every opening

Design note: curtains and films treat the symptom after the heat is already inside. External shading and the right glass specification stop it at the skin of the building — which is where our facade and beautification work starts.
